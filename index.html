Abstract

Within motorsport, high-performance braking systems are a technology based upon physics developments established through the years of material feasibility based on performance levels and because of effectiveness/safety in types - from Formula to GT to rallying - and all in-between to get recognized as the best of what's championed in racing. This paper explores high-performance braking systems as a technology with the world of motorsport, examining the physics, materials, electronics, and thermal management that enable consistent stopping potential across different racing categories.

Introduction

Over the years, the technology of braking systems has advanced in materials, physics and engineering in the various disciplines of Formula, GT and rally. The modern systems involve the use of advanced materials and aerothermal and electronic technologies, enabling the F1 cars to slow down from 200 mph to 60 mph in approximately three seconds (Mercedes AMG F1). Above 1,000°C, there is a big problem of thermal degradation and tolerance, which demands special research in this regard, like from Sabe Fluid Dynamics. Braking fundamentals are the same, but electronics like ABS vary among racing classes, adding to the variety in complexity and effectiveness. This paper thus addresses HABT technology in motorsport, discussing physics, materials, system evolution and thermal management, and the impact of racing classes on high performance road cars as well as supercars today.

Physics of Braking

When brakes are applied, kinetic energy is transformed to heat, which can place a lot of stress on the braking system. When reducing speed from 200 to 60 mph, F1 calipers and rotors have to generate a lot of heat (Mercedes AMG F1, 2023). When decelerating, tyre grip is the limiting factor for stopping power, whereas downforce (aerodynamic or mechanical) enhances tyre loading and thus braking power. Formula cars can produce more than 1000 lbs of down force and braking also moves weight forward. The temperature can reach 400°C to more than 1200°C (Sabe Fluid Dynamics, 2022), which means that it can cause high thermal and mechanical stress. Materials should not expand, split or be subjected to friction loads, and should perform consistently. So, if you want to resist these forces, the materials that have thermal resistance, stable performance coefficients and interaction between components are capable of resisting them.

Material Technology

Advanced high-performance braking systems rely on materials developed through racing, with carbon-carbon and carbon-ceramic discs favoured for their strength, heat resistance, longevity and low mass. Carbon-ceramic discs can weigh one-third to one-half as much as cast iron, reducing unsprung mass and rotational inertia to improve handling (SGL Carbon, 2000). F1 uses carbon-carbon discs and pads that regularly operate above 1,000°C, where steel would melt. Manufacturing involves resin infiltration, compression moulding, three-month carbonisation at nearly 2,500°C (4,532°F), followed by four weeks of CNC machining (Driver61, 2025). Modern discs contain around 1,200 cooling holes, machined to tolerances above 0.02 mm, while wear can reach 0.01 mm/km. These extreme tolerances and wear requirements demonstrate why F1 braking technology influences supercar systems, although GT and rally applications face greater cost and regulatory limitations.

Electronic Systems

Braking systems designed for comfort focus on giving assist over stopping force and cooling. ABS is banned in Formula One and drivers must control wheel lock-up by themselves (Mercedes AMG F1). Requires sensitivity because if they brake inconsistently then they lose lap time, if they don't help them then they give them direct mechanical feedback and they brake in a more predictable way into the high-speed corners. Brake migration also adjusts the braking force as a function of fuel load using hydraulic controls. GT cars can benefit from the Bosch Motorsport ABS M5, which can modulate braking thousands of times per second, and allows some controlled slip when it is useful for steering and slowing down (Bosch Motorsport). GT3 cars can have up to 12 ABS settings that can be changed depending on the conditions, enabling to adjust the car quickly in rainy conditions (Brembo). Rally cars need to be even more flexible, as the surface area continuously shifts and braking conditions are vital to the car's performance.

Brake-by-Wire Technology

A significant step forward in high performance braking is the introduction of Brake By Wire (BBW) in 2014 to replace the mechanical link from the rear brakes in Formula One. Energy recovery and integration is electronically controlled braking in BBW, while F1 hybrids can recover more than 250 kWh through braking, and need careful rear-axle pressure management (Racecar Engineering, 2015). Sensors convert the input from pedals into braking signals, taking into consideration changes in energy recovery during deceleration to ensure consistent braking and rear-axle stability. F1 and F.E are using BBW, but there are different integration requirements for fully electric systems to ensure braking performance and safe energy recovery. The electronic brake booster is also replacing hydraulic operation in GT vehicles that have also been fitted with BBW. Its significance has thus risen greatly in the era of hybrid and electric vehicles.

Thermal Management

Thermal factors can adversely affect the braking performance and safety since long exposure to heat can result in brake fade, boiling of fluids and degradation of materials. Cooling systems are then used to draw air through the calipers and pads to extract heat from the system and throw it to the tyre and road surface, especially in Formula One. Effective cooling is a must for brakes and suspension, as air can be pushed up to 400°C hotter than the air flowing in through the ventilation slots of the brake or suspension TotalSim. There are also critical operating temperature limits: around 400°C, carbon materials can start to glaze or degrade, and if the cooling rate is too high, depending on the humidity and wet conditions, this can also be a problem (Sabe Fluid Dynamics). Manufacturing tolerances should also consider wear and debris. Rally components, for example, may then be different in their design from those used on the road, because of the increased amount of debris and thermal cycling exposure. These stresses are further enhanced by the fast heating and uneven cooling. Therefore it is vital to have accurate tolerance management.

Comparison Across Racing Types

The problem is that braking solutions in different categories of racing are determined by practical tolerances and the requirements of each discipline, and are different from what is acceptable for road users. The highest deceleration performance is seen in Formula One with minimal weight, aerodynamic downforce and carbon-carbon brakes with brake-by-wire (BBW). Brake migration systems control braking distribution down the corners with accurate tolerances and even through chicanes. For endurance racing, robustness is of greater importance due to the additional braking systems used in GT cars, and the heavier these cars are, the greater the demands of the suspension. Rally cars have to be adaptable and safe, as the surface keeps shifting and when conditions like rain change the grip, rally cars must adjust. Also, with BBW, and heavily on regenerative braking, as the Formula E cars are fully electric, the adaptation of braking technology to each powertrain and racing environment is clearly seen.

Transition to Road Cars

This has led to an increased transfer of motorsport-derived solutions into the high performance road-car sub-systems, for example, super cars are using technology developed in motorsport. Lightweight and heat-resistant materials were transferred into commercial applications with the help of carbon-composite manufacturing, such as resin infiltration, (SGL Carbon). Likewise, ABS and electronic braking systems took innovations from motorsport and road use, with comfort and safety as well as performance being the key priorities. However, brake-by-wire has grown in importance as hybrid and electric systems have evolved. Such systems allow the braking, energy recovery and vehicle control to work together, instead of the traditional mechanical control. The transition illustrates how motorsport testing can uncover technologies that can be adapted for road-car applications which are reliable, safe and easy to integrate, within commercial requirements. This is why more and more breakthroughs in braking systems are being transferred from competitive racing to high-performance and hybrid cars.
