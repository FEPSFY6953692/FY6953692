Abstract

High-performance braking in motorsport, from Formula 1 to GT and rallying, depends on progress in physics, materials, electronics and thermal management. This paper covers how these technologies operate under different performance, safety and regulatory constraints, and how motorsport braking developments transfer to high-performance road cars and supercars.

Introduction

The braking materials, physics and engineering have developed across F1, GT and rallying. F1 cars, such as those of Mercedes-AMG PETRONAS, are capable of reducing their speed from 200 to 60 mph in approximately three seconds, due to advanced materials, aerothermal design and electronics (Mercedes-AMG PETRONAS F1 Team, 2019). Thermal degradation becomes appreciable at temperatures over 1,000°C, and specialised research like that from Sabe Fluid Dynamics (n.d.) is needed. Electronics regulations such as ABS cause a distinction in complexity between categories, although the physics is the same. This paper discusses the physics, materials, electronics, system evolution and thermal management of braking, and its transfer to road cars.

Physics of Braking

When braking, kinetic energy is transformed into heat by friction, causing heavy mechanical and thermal stresses on the system. When the car slows from 200 to 60 mph, a tremendous amount of heat is generated by the F1 calipers and rotors (Mercedes-AMG PETRONAS F1 Team, 2019). Aerodynamic downforce increases grip — F1 cars may have more than 1,000 lb of it — and heavy braking moves weight forward, where it is more easily held by the tyres. Materials should not expand, fracture or be overcome by frictional forces at 400°C, or even at places over 1,200°C (Sabe Fluid Dynamics, n.d.). Suitability depends on thermal resistance, stability of the friction coefficient, and disc-pad interaction.

Material Technology

Carbon-carbon and carbon-ceramic materials are popular in racing due to their strength, heat resistance, durability and light weight. Carbon-ceramic discs are approximately 1/3–1/2 the unsprung mass and rotational inertia of cast iron, which helps handling (SGL Carbon, n.d.). F1 discs and pads run even hotter, at over 1,000°C, and are resin-infused, compression-moulded, carbonised for nearly three months at close to 2,500°C, then CNC-machined over weeks (Driver61, 2025). Even with differing GT and rally cost and regulation limits, each disc has around 1,200 cooling holes, tolerances of no more than 0.02 mm, and wear of up to 0.01 mm/km.

Electronic Systems

Stopping power and driver control matter most for Formula One, unlike the comfort braking used on the road. There's no ABS — drivers must brake themselves and avoid wheel lock-up (Mercedes-AMG PETRONAS F1 Team, 2019), which is difficult but gives direct mechanical feedback and predictable braking into corners. Brake migration adjusts brake balance, via hydraulic control, for fuel load. GT cars can be fitted with Bosch Motorsport's ABS M5, which changes pressure thousands of times a second and allows controlled slip that enhances steering and braking, with up to 12 driver-selectable maps for conditions such as rain (Bosch Motorsport, n.d.). Rally cars must be even more versatile, as surface grip changes constantly and unpredictably.

Brake-by-Wire Technology

A great F1 development was brake-by-wire (BBW) in 2014. It combines the hydraulic rear brakes with the hybrid energy-recovery system, balancing the calipers and the MGU-K to deliver the right amount of braking power when the driver needs it; in heavy braking this can generate up to 350 kW that the energy-recovery system can use if managed properly, keeping rear-axle braking force under control (Racecar Engineering, 2025). A pedal sensor reads driver demand and accounts for available recovery, keeping feel and rear stability consistent regardless of battery charge. F1 and Formula E both use BBW, in different configurations for hybrid and all-electric cars, and GT cars use BBW and electronic brake boosters too.

Thermal Management

Thermal management matters because of brake fade, boiling brake fluid and material degradation. In F1, cooling ducts bring air through the calipers, pads and nearby suspension components — duct air is often hundreds of degrees cooler than the disc it cools (TotalSim, 2017). Carbon materials typically glaze or degrade around 400°C, and high cooling rates can also cause problems in wet conditions (Sabe Fluid Dynamics, n.d.). Wear, debris and thermal cycling are all important when setting tolerances; rally components are especially sensitive to temperature extremes and debris.

Comparison Across Racing Types

Braking varies depending on the categories of race, surface, endurance requirements and weight. F1 has the lowest weight, highest downforce, carbon-carbon brakes and BBW for the highest deceleration. GT cars are designed to be heavy and focus on durability rather than long duration. Rally cars have to adapt rapidly when conditions are changing quickly, particularly when wet. Formula E combines a fully electric powertrain with BBW.

Transition to Road Cars

High-performance road cars are increasingly influenced by motorsport technology. The lightweight, heat-resistant carbon composites used in racing are commercially produced, for instance by resin-infiltrated manufacturing (SGL Carbon, n.d.). Other examples of motorsport technology transferring to the road are ABS and electronic braking, which are more concerned with comfort, safety and reliability than performance. In hybrid and EV vehicles, BBW is increasingly critical, as it enables braking, energy recovery and vehicle control to work together. Technologies are tested and evaluated in motorsport before being applied to road vehicles with the necessary safety, reliability and integration.

Conclusion

Decades of advances in physics, materials, electronics and thermal management have come together to produce high-performance braking in motorsport, each advancing the other as regulations and race conditions change. Formula 1 shows what it takes to reach the edge of performance; GT and rally show endurance and constantly changing surfaces; and Formula E shows braking as part of a fully electric powertrain. Motorsport remains the breeding ground for ideas that trickle down to safer, more capable road cars and supercars.
