Abstract

From Formula, GT to rallying, high-performance braking systems in motorsport rely on the progress of physics, materials, electronics, thermal management and more. The paper covers the operation of these technologies for various performance, safety and regulatory constraints and how developments in motorsport are applied to high performance road cars and contemporary supercars.

Introduction

From Formula, GT and rallying, technology has evolved and improved braking materials, physics and engineering. F1 cars, such as Mercedes AMG F1, use advanced materials and aerothermal and electronic technologies to decelerate from 200 to 60 miles per hour in about three seconds. Thermal degradation and tolerance issues become significant at temperatures above 1,000°C and specialised research is needed, like Sabe Fluid Dynamics. While the principles of braking are the same, regulations for electronics like ABS make for differences in complexity and performance. The paper therefore focuses on HABT technology from physics, materials, electronics, system evolution and thermal management aspects, and looks at its effect on high performance road cars.

Physics of Braking

Braking involves kinetic energy being transformed into heat by friction, which can be very stressful on the system in terms of mechanical and thermal loading. F1 calipers and rotors have to release a tremendous amount of heat as they slow down from 200 to 60 mph (Mercedes AMG F1). Tyres' grip is the limiting factor when you brake, so aerodynamic or mechanical downforce increases the loading on tyres, which means they grip better. Formula cars can produce more than 1,000 lbs of downforce and braking will move the weight in the front. Resisting thermal expansion, fracturing and frictional loads is necessary for materials to perform consistently under temperatures that can reach 400°C up to more than 1,200°C (Sabe Fluid Dynamics). Thus, the suitability of materials is dependent on their thermal resistance, their stable performance coefficients and the interaction between materials.

Material Technology

The strength, heat resistance, durability and low weight of carbon-carbon and carbon-ceramic brake materials have made them ideal for racing, and it is for this reason that the two types of materials have been developed. The handling of carbon-ceramic discs is enhanced by their low unsprung masses and rotational inertia which is one-third to one-half that of cast iron (SGL Carbon). F1 carbon-carbon discs and pads are routinely used at temperatures in excess of 1000°C. They are manufactured by resin infusing, compression moulding, carbonising for nearly 3 months at nearly 2,500°C (4,532°F) followed by 4 weeks of CNC (Driver61) machining. Modern discs have approximately 1200 cooling holes and have tolerances greater than 0.02 mm. Wear can be up to 0.01 mm/km. The requirements give some idea of the impact they have on supercar braking, but that is not the case in GT and rally cars, where cost and regulation are different.

Electronic Systems

Formula One is about driver control and stopping performance, as opposed to comfort oriented road braking. Wheel lock-up is not allowed (ABS), drivers must control the wheel-locking themselves (Mercedes AMG F1). This is achieved with high sensitivity but also offers direct mechanical feedback and predictable braking into high-speed corners. Brake migration also controls the power of the break based on fuel load by hydraulic control. GT cars can be fitted with Bosch Motorsport's ABS M5, which adjusts braking thousands of times a second and allows controlled slip when it is beneficial for steering and braking (Bosch Motorsport). GT3 cars can offer up to 12 ABS settings to adjust to conditions, enabling quick changes in rain (Brembo). Rally cars need to be more adaptable as the conditions on the surfaces are constantly changing, and the braking conditions are unpredictable.

Brake-by-Wire Technology

The introduction of brake-by-wire (BBW) in 2014 was a significant development in F1.The brake-by-wire (BBW) system was a major development in F1 in 2014. The braking energy recovery system for BBW is an electronic system that integrates braking and energy recovery, with F1 hybrids recovering more than 250 kWh via braking and needing careful management of rear-axle pressure (Racecar Engineering). Pedal action is translated by sensors into braking signals which take into account varying energy recovery to ensure consistent braking and rear stability. F1 and Formula E are both using BBW, but with different integration of braking response and energy recovery for fully electric systems. Alongside hybrid and electric powertrains, BBW and electronic brake boosters have been introduced by GT vehicles, highlighting the significance of this technology for other applications.

Thermal Management

Thermal management is crucial due to the possibility of brake fade, boiling of fluids and degradation of material at excessive temperatures. Cooling systems direct airflow through calipers and pads to remove heat, particularly in F1. Cooling is important for both brakes and suspension (TotalSim) because the air can move up to 400°C faster than the air that is blown in. Carbon materials may start to glaze or even degrade around 400°C and high cooling rates can also cause problems under specific humidity and wet conditions (Sabe Fluid Dynamics). Tolerances need to include wear, debris and thermal cycling, however. The components in the rally must have specific resistance to debris, as well as to the rapid changes of temperature in both heat and cold, and therefore require a very specific tolerance management to be accurate.

Comparison Across Racing Types

The brake system must be different for each racing category due to the nature of the race, the surface and the endurance requirements, as well as the weight of the bike. The highest deceleration performance is achieved in Formula 1 with the low weight, aerodynamic down force, carbon-carbon brakes, BBW and brake migration, and individual control for corners and chicanes. Robustness is important to GT cars because they are heavier, which means they require more braking and suspension, especially when competing in endurance events. Rally cars need to be adaptable and safe as conditions and surface grip can change very quickly, particularly in wet conditions. The full electric nature of Formula E is combining with BBW, highlighting the need to adapt braking technology to other power trains and other environments.

Transition to Road Cars

Motorsport developments are being increasingly moved to high performance road-car systems. Commercial applications of lightweight, heat-resistant carbon composites developed in the racing arena, such as resin-infiltrated manufacturing, are in use (SGL Carbon). ABS and electronic braking systems were also another example of motorsport technology being transferred to road applications, where comfort, safety and reliability were also key factors as well as performance. With hybrid and electric powertrains, BBW has come into increasing importance as it enables braking, energy recovery and vehicle control to work together. Motorsport is thus a testing ground for technologies that can be transferred to road vehicles with acceptable levels of safety, reliability and integration for commercial use.
