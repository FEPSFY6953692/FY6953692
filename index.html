00 — Abstract

High-performance braking in motorsport, from Formula 1 to GT and rallying, depends on progress in physics, materials, electronics and thermal management. This paper covers how these technologies operate under different performance, safety and regulatory constraints, and how motorsport braking developments transfer to high-performance road cars and supercars.

01 — Introduction

Introduction

Braking materials, physics and engineering have evolved across Formula 1, GT and rallying. F1 cars, such as those run by Mercedes-AMG PETRONAS, use advanced materials, aerothermal design and electronics to decelerate from 200 to 60 mph in about three seconds (Mercedes-AMG PETRONAS F1 Team, 2019). Thermal degradation becomes significant above 1,000°C, requiring specialised research such as that from Sabe Fluid Dynamics (n.d.). While the underlying physics is shared, electronics regulations such as those governing ABS create real differences in complexity across categories. This paper examines braking through physics, materials, electronics, system evolution and thermal management, and its transfer to road cars.

02 — Physics of Braking

Physics of Braking

Braking converts kinetic energy into heat through friction, placing heavy mechanical and thermal loads on the system. F1 calipers and rotors release enormous heat as the car slows from 200 to 60 mph (Mercedes-AMG PETRONAS F1 Team, 2019). Tyre grip is the limiting factor, so aerodynamic downforce — F1 cars can exceed 1,000 lb — improves grip, while heavy braking shifts weight forward. Materials must resist thermal expansion, fracturing and frictional loads at 400°C and, in places, over 1,200°C (Sabe Fluid Dynamics, n.d.). Suitability depends on thermal resistance, a stable friction coefficient, and how the disc and pad interact.

03 — Material Technology

Material Technology

Carbon-carbon and carbon-ceramic materials are widely used in racing for their strength, heat resistance, durability and low weight. Carbon-ceramic discs have roughly one-third to one-half the unsprung mass and rotational inertia of cast iron, improving handling (SGL Carbon, n.d.). F1 carbon-carbon discs and pads exceed 1,000°C in operation and are resin-infused, compression-moulded, carbonised for close to three months at around 2,500°C, then CNC-machined over several weeks (Driver61, 2025). Discs contain around 1,200 cooling holes, hold tolerances tighter than 0.02 mm, and wear at up to 0.01 mm/km — figures that shape supercar systems even where GT and rally run to different cost and regulatory limits.

04 — Electronic Systems

Electronic Systems

Formula One prioritises driver control and stopping performance over the comfort-oriented braking used on the road. There is no ABS, so drivers must control wheel-locking themselves (Mercedes-AMG PETRONAS F1 Team, 2019); this is demanding, but gives direct mechanical feedback and predictable braking into corners. Brake migration adjusts brake balance for fuel load through hydraulic control. GT cars can run Bosch Motorsport's ABS M5, which adjusts pressure thousands of times a second and allows controlled slip where it benefits steering and braking, offering up to twelve driver-selectable maps for changing conditions such as rain (Bosch Motorsport, n.d.). Rally cars need to be more adaptable still, since surface grip changes constantly and unpredictably.

05 — Brake-by-Wire Technology

Brake-by-Wire Technology

Brake-by-wire (BBW), introduced in 2014, was a major F1 development. It integrates the hydraulic rear brakes with the hybrid energy-recovery system, arbitrating between the calipers and the MGU-K so rear braking force matches driver demand; F1 power units can harvest up to 350 kW during heavy braking, requiring careful management of rear-axle pressure (Racecar Engineering, 2025). A pedal sensor reads driver demand and accounts for available recovery, keeping braking feel and rear stability consistent as battery charge changes. F1 and Formula E both use BBW, integrated differently for hybrid and fully electric powertrains, and GT cars have also adopted BBW and electronic brake boosters.

06 — Thermal Management

Thermal Management

Thermal management is crucial given the risk of brake fade, boiling brake fluid and material degradation. Cooling ducts direct airflow to the calipers and pads, critical in F1 for both brakes and nearby suspension, since duct air is typically hundreds of degrees cooler than the disc it cools (TotalSim, 2017). Carbon materials can glaze or degrade around 400°C, and high cooling rates can also cause problems in wet conditions (Sabe Fluid Dynamics, n.d.). Tolerances must account for wear, debris and thermal cycling; rally components especially need resistance to debris and rapid heat–cold swings.

07 — Comparison Across Racing Types

Comparison Across Racing Types

Braking differs across categories with the race, surface, endurance needs and weight. F1 achieves the highest deceleration through low weight, downforce, carbon-carbon brakes and BBW. GT cars, being heavier, prioritise robustness over long stints. Rally cars must adapt fast as grip changes suddenly, especially in the wet. Formula E pairs a fully electric powertrain with BBW.

08 — Transition to Road Cars

Transition to Road Cars

Motorsport developments increasingly carry over into high-performance road cars. Lightweight, heat-resistant carbon composites developed for racing, such as resin-infiltrated manufacturing, are now in commercial production (SGL Carbon, n.d.). ABS and electronic braking are further examples of motorsport technology transferred to the road, where comfort, safety and reliability sit alongside performance. With hybrid and electric powertrains, BBW is increasingly important, letting braking, energy recovery and vehicle control work together. Motorsport therefore acts as a proving ground for technologies later transferred to road vehicles with acceptable safety, reliability and integration.

09 — Conclusion

Conclusion

High-performance braking in motorsport is the product of decades of progress across physics, materials, electronics and thermal management, each pushing the others forward as regulations and race conditions change. Formula 1 shows what is possible at the limit of performance; GT and rally adapt the same principles to endurance and unpredictable surfaces; and Formula E shows how braking integrates with a fully electric powertrain. Motorsport remains the proving ground from which these strategies filter down into safer, more capable road cars and supercars.
